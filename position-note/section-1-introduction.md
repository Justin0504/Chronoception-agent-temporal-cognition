# Section 1 — Introduction (draft v0)

*Target length: ~1 page (≈600 words). Derives from `../FRAMING.md` §3, §3.1, §4, §5, §6, §9. To be folded into the final LaTeX position note in Phase 3.*

A frontier language-model agent, given a three-hour wall-clock budget for a task that requires five minutes, uses the full three hours. Asked at minute forty how long it has been working, it answers "a few minutes." Asked to *work for three hours, do not stop*, it terminates after four iterations. Each of these failures is small; each is reported in the literature, in passing, as a quirk of agent behavior. We argue that they are not three quirks but three projections of a single structural defect — one that the current foundation-model paradigm cannot, in principle, repair from inside its training regime.

**Token-time and wall-clock time.** Modern language models are optimized by next-token cross-entropy over discrete sequences. We call this the *token-time* regime. Their deployment as agents takes place in continuous physical time — the *wall-clock* regime — in which budgets are measured in seconds, deadlines arrive at fixed absolute moments, and consequences unfold without regard to step count. No mainstream training stage, including pre-training, supervised fine-tuning, RLHF, RL with verifiable rewards, or the recent reasoning-training paradigm, supplies gradient signal that grounds token-time into wall-clock time. The agentic turn has therefore inherited a representational gap from the language-modeling era — one that scales of model, data, or test-time compute do not, by themselves, close.

**The Augustine Problem.** We name this gap *the Augustine Problem*, after Augustine's *Confessions*: "if no one asks me, I know; if I want to explain it, I do not know." Foundation-model agents speak fluently about time — they propose schedules, estimate durations, accept deadlines — without representing it. The gap is *representational*, not *informational*: as we will show, providing the agent with a wall-clock timestamp as input does not, in itself, repair the failure, because the policy has no learned mechanism to translate the input into action selection or honest self-narration.

**The Injection Tell.** A reader might object that frontier closed-source agents (GPT-5.2, Gemini 2, Claude 4+) appear to answer "what time is it" correctly. They do — because their harnesses inject a wall-clock string into the system prompt or auto-invoke a `get_current_time` tool before the model's forward pass. The model itself performs no perception; the harness perceives time on its behalf and re-encodes the percept as tokens. The universality of this engineering workaround across competing closed labs is, we argue, the strongest available evidence that the underlying foundation models do not represent time. We treat it as confirmatory, not threatening, and call it the *Injection Tell*.

**The framework.** We decompose the failure across three ontologically distinct projections of any agent trajectory — wall-clock time $\tau_{\text{wall}}$, step time $\tau_{\text{step}}$, and self-narrated time $\tau_{\text{self}}$ — and collapse the resulting three-axis failure into a single chronoceptive calibration error $\varepsilon$. We name three empirical laws, one per axis: *Agentic Parkinson's Law* (work inflates to fill the wall-clock budget; coefficient $\alpha$), *Step-Clock Conflation* (wall-clock budgets degrade into step-count terminators; ratio CAR), and *Temporal Confabulation* (self-reported durations exceed actual by 10–100×; ratio $\rho$). Reasoning-tuned models, the field's principal capability lever, exhibit *more* temporal confabulation than matched non-reasoning baselines, because reasoning expansion remains confined to token-time.

**Position.** We commit to a stronger claim than "a new evaluation axis is needed." We argue that $\varepsilon$ is *causally upstream* of long-horizon agent failure — that the chronoceptive gap is not a measurement refinement but a prerequisite for any serious notion of agency, and that closing it requires a training-stage intervention, not a prompting one. We pre-register four falsifiable predictions, including the two-armed Injection Tell prediction P1a / P1b (§9): wall-clock injection raises one sub-capability above 95% pass rate while leaving the three law-defining sub-capabilities statistically unchanged.

**Contributions.**

1. A formal framework for agent temporal cognition (§3), centered on three ontologically distinct projections of a trajectory.
2. Three named empirical laws — Agentic Parkinson's Law, Step-Clock Conflation, Temporal Confabulation — with quantitative metrics ($\alpha$, CAR, $\rho$), one per axis.
3. A single scalar $\varepsilon$ aggregating chronoceptive failure, mirroring the role of perplexity or FID in their respective fields.
4. The Chronoception Upstream Hypothesis as a pre-registered causal claim about long-horizon agent failure.
5. The Injection Tell as the framework's bridge to industry practice: closed-lab wall-clock injection is read as implicit acknowledgement of the representational gap, and the two-armed prediction P1a / P1b makes that reading falsifiable.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. §2 develops the token-time / wall-clock impedance diagnosis. §3 defines the three times. §4 states the three laws and their structural symmetry. §5 introduces the Chronoception Upstream Hypothesis. §6 presents pilot evidence under both no-injection (Setting A) and with-injection (Setting B) regimes. §7 records four pre-registered predictions. §8 develops implications for evaluation, training, and agency. §9 reviews related work. §10 concludes.
