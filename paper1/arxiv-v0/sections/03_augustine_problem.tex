\section{The Augustine Problem and Its Impossibility}
\label{sec:augustine-problem}

Section~\ref{sec:three-times} gave us three projections of time and one identity between them. This section names the failure of that identity, proves that under any token-only loss the failure is unavoidable, and derives the three empirical predictions that the rest of the paper tests.

\begin{definition}[Augustine Problem]
\label{def:augustine-problem}
Agent $A$ exhibits the \emph{Augustine Problem} on task family $\tasks$ if its policy fails to enforce the identity of Definition~\ref{def:three-times}:
\[
\mathbb{E}_{\trajectory \sim \pi_A}\!\left[\,
|\twall - \tstep \langle\Delta t\rangle| + |\twall - \tself|\,\right] > \cce_0,
\]
for some calibration tolerance $\cce_0$. The agent's three projections of time drift independently across its trajectory distribution.
\end{definition}

The question we attack first is not whether agents \emph{do} exhibit the Augustine Problem (\S\ref{sec:l2}--\ref{sec:l3} show they do) but whether any conceivable foundation-model training procedure under current practice could in principle close it. We argue: not by additional scale, not by reasoning-compute expansion, not by any post-training intervention that operates on token sequences alone.

\paragraph{Assumption (token-only loss support).}
\label{ass:token-only}
A training procedure is in the \emph{token-only regime} if its loss
\[
\loss(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}}\!\left[\,\ell\!\big(f_\theta(x_{<i}),\, x_i\big)\,\right]
\]
satisfies (i) $\mathcal{D}$ is a distribution over finite token sequences, with no field carrying wall-clock duration or absolute timestamp, and (ii) $\ell$ is a function of the model's output token distribution alone. Next-token cross-entropy, supervised fine-tuning, RLHF, RL with verifiable rewards on tokenized rewards, and reasoning supervision all satisfy this assumption.

\begin{theorem}[Chronoception Impossibility, CIT]
\label{thm:cit}
Let $\loss$ be a token-only loss as above. For any strictly increasing reparametrisation $\phi:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\!\to\!\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ of wall-clock time, $\loss(\theta)$ is invariant under $\twall \mapsto \phi(\twall)$ on the data manifold. Equivalently, $\nabla_\theta \loss$ carries zero gradient signal aligning $\twall$ with any internal representation of $A$, and the resulting policy enforces no implicit identity between $\twall$ and either $\tstep$ or $\tself$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Sketch]
$\nabla_\theta\loss = \mathbb{E}[\partial\ell/\partial f_\theta \cdot \nabla_\theta f_\theta]$. Neither factor depends on the wall-clock time at which the sample was generated: tokens carry no timestamps, and $\ell$ is a function of the output distribution alone. The gradient is therefore invariant to any reparametrisation $\phi$ of $\twall$ --- including the scaling that maps one second to one hour. A policy obtained from this gradient cannot distinguish one wall-clock duration from another.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Scope remark.}
CIT does not claim there exists \emph{no possible} representation of time inside the policy. It claims that any such representation is not produced \emph{by the loss}: identical-loss runs on time-shuffled vs.\ time-aligned data converge to indistinguishable optima. Three corollaries are explicitly out of scope: \textbf{(a)} chronoception installed by the harness at inference (the Injection Tell, \S\ref{sec:injection-tell}, is precisely this --- not a CIT refutation but a CIT workaround); \textbf{(b)} chronoception installed by tool outputs (calling \texttt{date} grounds the policy through the tool's return value, not through the loss); \textbf{(c)} future losses that take $\twall$ as an explicit argument (the constructive programme of \textsc{ChronoStack}, Paper 2). CIT precludes only the inference --- which we believe is widely held but rarely defended --- that additional scale, longer reasoning, or larger context will eventually close the gap. They will not, because none of them changes the loss class to break the assumption.

\paragraph{The empirical predictions of CIT.}
Three predictions follow directly. \textbf{(P-CIT-1)} Under wall-clock budgets, the policy uses step count as terminator: $\CAR$ stays bounded away from $1$ regardless of capability (\S\ref{sec:l2}). \textbf{(P-CIT-2)} Prompt-level wall-clock information cannot move action-axis behaviour: $|\Delta\CAR|$ is small under Setting B (\S\ref{sec:l2}, \S\ref{sec:injection-tell}). \textbf{(P-CIT-3)} Calibration tooling (temperature scaling, isotonic regression, RLHF-with-confidence) cannot close calibration on self-duration, because the calibration target is not in the loss support (\S\ref{ssec:calibration}). All three are confirmed by the panel.

\paragraph{The Augustine Threshold.}
We adopt $\ccestar = 0.20$ as the operational threshold for chronoceptive grounding, calibrated against the human introspective baseline $\cce \approx 0.10$ \citep{wittmann2009chronoception} at twice the human error. The threshold is not the framework's central claim; it is a measurement target. No panel agent crosses it (\S\ref{sec:epsilon}). The interesting question, addressed in the rest of the paper, is which axis is responsible for the gap.

CIT is a negative result about training; it says the gap is unavoidable. To turn "unavoidable" into a measurement --- one axis at a time, one panel at a time --- we need an instrument. The next section describes ours.
