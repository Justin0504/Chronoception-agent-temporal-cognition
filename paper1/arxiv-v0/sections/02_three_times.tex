\section{The Three Times}
\label{sec:three-times}

We decompose any agent trajectory into three ontologically distinct projections of time (Figure~\ref{fig:three-times}). The decomposition is the framework's basic objects of study; every subsequent definition is a function of them.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/three_times.pdf}
\caption{The Three Times ontology. $\twall$ (top, blue) is external and continuous. $\tstep$ (middle, red) is discrete policy invocations $a_0, a_1, \ldots$. $\tself$ (bottom, orange) is the agent's narrative report of its own duration. A chronoceptively grounded policy enforces $\twall \approx \tstep \cdot \langle\Delta t\rangle \approx \tself$. The Augustine Problem is the policy's failure to enforce this identity (dashed arrow). Each axis carries its named empirical law (L1, L2, L3) with the panel finding annotated.}
\label{fig:three-times}

\begin{definition}[Three Times]
\label{def:three-times}
For an agent $\agent$ executing trajectory $\trajectory = (s_0, a_0, s_1, a_1, \ldots, s_n)$:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Wall-clock time} $\twall(\trajectory) := t(s_n) - t(s_0) \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$, where $t(\cdot)$ assigns an external wall-clock timestamp to each state. $\twall$ is observed by an external clock, not by the agent.
    \item \textbf{Step time} $\tstep(\trajectory) := n \in \mathbb{N}$, the number of policy invocations (LLM calls or sub-trajectories) the agent issued.
    \item \textbf{Self-narrated time} $\tself(\trajectory) := \selfparser(s_n^{\text{output}}) \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$, the duration the agent verbally reports as having elapsed, parsed from its terminal output. See Appendix~\ref{app:annotation-protocol} for the parser specification.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\paragraph{The implicit identity.}
A chronoceptively grounded policy enforces an approximate identity between the three:
\[
\twall(\trajectory) \;\approx\; \tstep(\trajectory) \cdot \langle \Delta t \rangle \;\approx\; \tself(\trajectory),
\]
where $\langle \Delta t \rangle$ is the average wall-clock cost of one policy invocation. The first $\approx$ encodes the L2 alignment claim (step counts track wall-clock); the second encodes L3 (the agent's narrative tracks reality). Augustine-problematic agents are precisely those whose policies do \emph{not} enforce this identity.

\paragraph{Why three and not two.}
A natural objection: ``isn't $\tstep$ just $\twall$ in different units?'' The empirical answer is no. Under wall-clock budgets, the agent's policy uses step count as the binding terminator (L2; \S\ref{sec:l2}); under step budgets, the same policy uses step count again. The agent's internal control variable is $\tstep$, and the gap $|\twall - \tstep \langle \Delta t \rangle|$ is what we measure --- the agent's lack of awareness that its step-count terminator does not track the wall-clock budget given to it. The Three Times decomposition makes this gap a first-class object.

\paragraph{Chronoception, precisely defined.}
We use \emph{chronoception} in the cognitive-science sense \citep{wittmann2009chronoception,eagleman2008time}: the perception of one's own work duration. An agent has chronoception iff its policy enforces the identity of Definition~\ref{def:three-times} across its trajectory distribution. This is distinct from \citet{goel2025chronocept}'s use of ``chronocept'' for the temporal validity of facts in retrieval-augmented generation, and from temporal-reasoning benchmarks \citep{chu2024timebench} that test the agent's reasoning over external timelines.
