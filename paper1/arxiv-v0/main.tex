\begin{abstract}
Large language model agents are trained on token sequences but act in wall-clock time. We ask, empirically, whether they perceive their own time. We formalise a \emph{three-times} ontology --- wall-clock time $\twall$, step time $\tstep$, and self-narrated time $\tself$ --- define the \emph{Augustine Problem} as the failure of an agent's policy to enforce the identity linking them, and introduce \textsc{ChronoBench}, a diagnostic benchmark over nine sub-capabilities across the three axes. We evaluate 7 frontier agents from 3 providers (OpenAI, Anthropic, open-source self-hosted) under both no-injection and with-injection settings, across roughly 1{,}200 trajectories. We report four primary empirical findings: (1) every panel agent uses $\leq 5\%$ of wall-clock budgets given (\emph{L2 Step-Clock Conflation}, unmoved by injection); (2) median self-narration error $|\confab|$ closes monotonically with capability scaling ($+1.12 \to +0.07$ across 5 generations), while $\CAR$ does not; (3) consumer web-chat products inject today's date into system prompts at 100\% rate (verbatim leaked-system-prompt evidence), while developer-tool products do not; (4) no panel agent crosses the Augustine threshold $\ccestar = 0.20$ because L2 alone exceeds it for every model. We close with the Agentic Timeline Hypothesis: chronoception is the rate-limiting capability for autonomous agent deployment, with pre-registered prediction P12 testable against horizon-stratified benchmarks. We release \textsc{ChronoBench}, the Injection Atlas, model traces, and a \texttt{pip}-installable evaluation package.
\end{abstract}
