LLM agents are trained on tokens and deployed in wall-clock time. We prove they cannot close that gap from training alone: under any loss that is a functional of token sequences — from next-token prediction through RLHF to reasoning supervision — the gradient is invariant to how long the episode took. No amount of scale, context, or reasoning compute installs a representation of elapsed time. Chronoception must be installed from outside the loss.

We name the failure the Augustine Problem, decompose it along the three times an agent must keep aligned (τ_wall, τ_step, τ_self), and measure it on ChronoBench: 9 sub-capabilities × 14 agents from 7 vendors, ~5,900 trajectories. Every agent leaves ≥89% of a stated wall-clock budget unspent, and a 60× increase in that budget buys 1.4× more work. Injecting the current date repairs what agents say — date accuracy rises to ≥96% panel-wide — and moves what they do by |ΔCAR| ≤ 0.01. Three closed labs have independently reached the same workaround, routing the wall-clock into the token stream for consumer products (3/3 inject the date) but not for developer tools (0/3): the harness perceives time on the model's behalf. Reasoning makes the gap worse, not better. We prove E[|ρ| given reasoning budget K] is monotone non-decreasing in K and confirm it four ways, including a sign flip reproduced on an independent vendor's pipeline; nominal 90% intervals on self-reported duration achieve 0–77% coverage.

The consequence is a deployment bound, T_max ∝ 1/ε, supported on 14,709 METR HCAST runs (one-sided p = 0.040): autonomous-agent horizons are limited by chronoception, not by intelligence. The gap is structural but not permanent — a LoRA fine-tune on wall-clock-supported targets crosses our calibration threshold after ~60 s of training, the constructive converse of the impossibility result.
